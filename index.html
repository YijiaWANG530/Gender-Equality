INTERACTIVE MODEL

The Compounding Gap

How small differences in opportunity can create larger inequalities over time.

This interactive model explores how repeated differences in promotion opportunity can affect representation and salary growth across a career.

The project uses a simplified model to show the cumulative impact of differences in promotion opportunities. It is intended to help explain the mechanism, rather than predict outcomes in real companies or directly prove discrimination.

RESEARCH BASIS

Why model a small repeated gap?

Research on gender inequality at work suggests that disadvantages can accumulate across multiple stages of an employee's career, including evaluation, promotion and compensation.

Studies have also documented gender differences in promotion outcomes in particular workplace samples. However, the size of these differences varies across occupations, organizations and populations.

About the numbers in this model

The default 50% and 45% promotion probabilities are illustrative, not estimates of the true promotion rates of men and women. They are chosen to demonstrate how a small repeated difference can compound across several stages. All parameters can be adjusted.

Promotion Pipeline

Adjust the promotion probabilities and see how the number of people in each group changes across multiple career stages.

Mathematical model

Nk = N0pk

Why this model?

Let Xk represent the number of people who reach stage k. The model uses the expected headcount Nk = E[Xk]. If each person has the same promotion probability p at every stage, then Nk+1 = pNk. Starting from 1,000 people, repeating this transition gives Nk = 1000pk. These values are expected outcomes under the model, not exact predictions of how many people will be promoted in a real organization.

Symbols

N0 Starting population
p Promotion probability at each stage
k Number of promotion stages
Nk Expected headcount reaching stage k
How to read this

Both groups start from the same population. The model focuses on how repeated differences in promotion probability change the relative size of the two groups over time.

Equalizing opportunities at a later stage does not restore people who did not advance in earlier stages. This scenario is a simplified model comparison, not a prediction of the effect of a real policy.

02

Representation Ratio

Repeated differences in promotion probability can produce a larger relative gap between the two groups across stages.

Mathematical model

Rk = NB,k NA,k = ( pB pA )k

Why this model?

Both groups follow the same promotion model: N(A,k) = N(0)p(A)^k and N(B,k) = N(0)p(B)^k. Dividing Group B by Group A cancels the common starting population N(0), leaving R(k) = (p(B)/p(A))^k. The ratio therefore isolates the cumulative effect of the difference in promotion probabilities.

Symbols

NA,k Group A population after k stages
NB,k Group B population after k stages
pA, pB Promotion probabilities for the two groups
Rk Group B population relative to Group A after k stages
How to read this

If Rk = 0.59, this means that Group B's expected headcount is 59.05% of Group A's expected headcount. It is calculated as Group B divided by Group A, not as Group B's share of the combined total.

Final representation ratio 59.05%

For every 100 people in Group A, approximately 59 remain in Group B.

Group B's promotion probability is 5 percentage points lower than Group A's. After the selected stages, Group B's expected population is 59.05% of Group A's.

03

Salary Growth

This section uses illustrative currency values to show how access to higher career levels can correspond to higher salary levels in the model.

Mathematical model

Sj = S0(1 + g)j

Why this model?

The model assumes that each promotion increases salary by the same proportional rate g. Each promotion therefore multiplies the previous salary by (1 + g), giving S(j+1) = (1 + g)S(j). After j promotions, repeated multiplication gives S(j) = S(0)(1 + g)^j. This is a proportional-growth model rather than a curve fitted to salary data.

Symbols

S0 Starting salary
g Salary growth rate per promotion
j Number of promotions
Sj Salary after j promotions
How to read this

The currency values in this section are illustrative model values rather than annual or cumulative income. The model does not assume unequal pay for the same role. It shows how differences in access to promotion can affect access to higher salary levels.

The salary is the same at the same level, but the expected number of people who can reach that level may differ. Each row represents the same group of people at different stages, so the numbers should not be added together.

MODEL ASSUMPTIONS

What does this simplified model assume?

Both groups begin with the same normalized starting population of 1,000 people. This value is used for illustration and does not represent the size of a real organization.

ach group is assigned a constant promotion probability across all stages. The default probabilities are illustrative values rather than estimates from a specific organization or population. In real workplaces, promotion rates may vary across job levels and career stages.

Population values represent expected outcomes under the model rather than predictions of the exact number of people promoted in a real workforce.

The salary model assumes the same proportional salary increase after each promotion. It illustrates access to higher salary levels rather than unequal pay for the same role.

The model does not include competition for a fixed number of promotion positions, new entrants, or repeated attempts to gain promotion.

Not advancing to the next stage in this model does not mean that a person is fired or leaves the organization. It only means that they do not move to the next modeled career stage.

04

Why This Matters

Gender inequality does not always emerge from one dramatic act of discrimination. Small differences in access to opportunities can accumulate across multiple stages of a career.

This idea is sometimes described as cumulative disadvantage: repeated small differences can eventually produce substantial differences in representation and income.

Transparent criteria

Make promotion standards clear and consistent.

Monitor opportunities

Examine whether access to career opportunities is distributed fairly.

Act early

Address small differences before they compound over time.
